The rain hammered against the salt-streaked windows of the *SS Meridian*, a relentless drumming that seemed to drown out the silence of the waiting room. We had been on the dock for forty-five minutes, the departure time long since etched into the misty air. The captain’s announcement had been brief, a muffled crackle over the intercom: "Engine trouble. Indeterminate delay."

Indeterminate. That was the word that sparked it.

It started with old Mr. Henderson, a man who usually only spoke to his dog. He turned to the woman next to him, a young girl with a soaked umbrella, and said, "I’ve seen the other side. It’s not a town. It’s a museum. They keep the ghosts in glass cases."

The girl blinked, her eyes wide. "A museum?"

"Best in the world," Henderson said, a strange, jagged smile creeping across his face. "The exhibits are still warm."

The girl looked at me, her expression shifting from confusion to a sudden, conspiratorial gleam. She leaned in. "Actually," she whispered, "I’m not a passenger. I’m the ghost. I’m waiting for my case to be unlocked."

I stared at her, the cold seeping into my bones. "You’re joking," I said, though my voice lacked conviction.

"Am I?" She laughed, a dry, brittle sound. "Look at your watch. It’s stopped. It’s been stopped for ten years. You just don’t remember."

I looked down. The second hand was indeed frozen at twelve. Panic, cold and sharp, pricked at my throat. I looked around the room. The other passengers were no longer looking at their phones or the window. They were looking at each other, eyes bright with a new, illicit energy.

The man in the corner, who had been reading a newspaper, folded it neatly and stood up. "I’m not a man," he announced to the room. "I’m the engine. I’m the one that’s broken. And I’m tired."

A ripple of laughter went through the group, but it wasn’t joyful. It was nervous, jagged. The lie had infected them. The delay had stripped away the polite veneer of strangers, revealing a desperate need to reshape reality into something more manageable, or perhaps, more terrifying.

"I’m the rain," the woman by the window said, touching the glass. "I’m the one keeping you here. I’m not going to stop."

I wanted to correct them. I wanted to say that the engine was just a mechanical failure, that the watch was broken, that the town was just a town. But the air in the room had grown thick, charged with the electricity of their fabrications. The truth felt fragile, easily shattered, while their lies were solid, warm, and shared.

I looked at Henderson, who was now staring at the empty space where his dog should have been. "The dog is the key," he said softly. "He knows the way out."

I looked at the girl, then at the man who claimed to be the engine. I realized then that the delay wasn’t a punishment. It was an invitation. We were no longer waiting for the ferry. We were waiting for the story to end, and for the first time in years, I was ready to believe anything.
