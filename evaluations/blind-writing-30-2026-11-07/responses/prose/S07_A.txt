The morning air was thick with the scent of wild thyme and damp earth, a perfume that usually signaled the height of the harvest. Elias stood before the row of cedar boxes, his gloves heavy and stained with propolis, the sticky resin that bound the hive together. He had come to check the frames, to count the brood and ensure the queen was laying true, but his hands trembled in a way that had nothing to do with the cold.

He had to tell them. It was a foolish thought, a whisper of anthropomorphism that had crept into his mind during the long, silent hours of the funeral. But as he lifted the inner cover, the hiss of the swarm rising like a wave of static in his ears, he felt the weight of the unspoken truth pressing against his chest. His mother had died three days ago. The house was quiet now, a hollow echo of the laughter that used to fill the kitchen.

Elias didn’t speak. Bees do not understand English, or grief, or the concept of a eulogy. They understood scent, vibration, and the chemical language of pheromones. He knew that if he spoke, it would be only for himself, a desperate attempt to bridge the gap between his human sorrow and their insect existence. Instead, he simply opened the hive.

The bees reacted with their usual urgency. They buzzed, a low, resonant hum that vibrated in his teeth. They were busy, indifferent to the tragedy that had befallen their keeper. They cared for the larvae, they guarded the entrance, they danced to indicate the distance to the clover fields. They did not know that the hands that fed them were now empty of purpose.

Elias reached in to lift a frame. The wood was warm from the bodies of thousands. He saw the honey, golden and thick, capped and ready for extraction. It looked like liquid sunlight, preserved and perfect. For a moment, he felt a surge of anger. How could they be so focused on the future, on the storage of sweetness, when the past had just been severed? He wanted to shake the hive, to make them feel the sudden absence, the cold void where warmth used to be.

But he didn’t. He gently slid the frame back into place. He watched a worker bee land on his glove, its legs brushing against the leather. It did not look at him. It did not mourn. It simply worked. In that small, blind creature, Elias found a strange, terrifying comfort. Life continued. The cycle did not pause for the dead. The bees did not need to be told; they only needed to survive.

He closed the lid, the weight of it settling onto the hive. The buzzing continued, a constant, indifferent drone. Elias walked back to his truck, the smell of thyme clinging to his clothes. He did not cry. He just drove, the engine of the truck mirroring the hum of the hive, a mechanical heartbeat in a world that was moving on without him.
