The classroom smelled of dry-erase markers and stale apple juice. I sat at my desk, my fingers nervously twisting the hem of my sweater, while Mr. Henderson tapped his watch against the whiteboard. "Who’s up next? Maya?"

My stomach dropped. I stood up, my chair scraping loudly against the linoleum. Twenty pairs of eyes turned toward me. Twenty pairs of eyes that had never looked up from a floor that wasn’t solid.

"My mom is an astronaut," I said. The words felt small, insignificant, like a pebble dropped into a vast ocean.

A few kids snickered. "Is she in space right now?" one asked, his voice laced with the specific skepticism of a ten-year-old who believes in magic but not in physics.

"No," I said, trying to sound braver than I felt. "She’s on a mission. It’s… it’s like being a doctor, an engineer, and a pilot all at once, but the hospital is a metal can floating around the Earth."

I tried to explain the silence. That was the hardest part. How do you describe the silence of orbit? It isn’t empty; it’s heavy. It’s the kind of quiet that presses against your eardrums. I told them that her job wasn’t about floating in zero gravity, even though that’s what they wanted to hear. It was about fixing a leaky valve at 3 a.m. while the sun rose every ninety minutes. It was about growing lettuce in a tiny, glowing box and watching it turn yellow because the humidity was wrong. It was about missing my birthday, not because she was dead, but because she was busy keeping a machine alive that cost billions of dollars and held the dreams of thousands of people.

"She’s lonely," I admitted, surprising myself. The class went quiet. I hadn’t expected that word.

"Lonely?" Mr. Henderson asked, his eyebrows raised.

"Yes," I said. "She’s the only person in a bubble, looking down at a world that’s so big it makes you feel like a dust mote. She talks to me through a screen that lags by three seconds. I say 'I love you,' and I have to wait for the echo. It’s like talking to a ghost."

I looked at the other kids, really looked at them. They had pets, they had siblings, they had parents who came to soccer games. They didn’t know what it felt like to hug someone who was forty thousand miles away. They didn’t know the specific ache of missing someone who was technically right there, just above the clouds, watching the same stars I did, but separated by an atmosphere that felt like a wall of glass.

"So," I finished, sitting back down, "she’s not a superhero. She’s just a woman who fixes things in the sky so we can keep breathing down here."

Mr. Henderson nodded slowly. "Thank you, Maya. That’s… a very unique perspective."

I returned to my seat, my heart still hammering. I didn’t care about the snickers anymore. I just wanted to go home and check my phone, hoping that the little green icon would light up, reminding me that despite the distance, the silence, and the lag, she was still there.
