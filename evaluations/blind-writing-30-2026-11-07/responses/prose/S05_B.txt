The red light of the answering machine blinks in the dim kitchen, a small, rhythmic pulse in the quiet of the house. Elias stands before it, his hands resting on the cool edge of the counter. He has rehearsed this moment a thousand times in his head, walking through the script of his own extinction, but now that the time has come, the words refuse to take shape. He is the last. The last to speak the tongue of the river valley, the last to hear the specific click of the consonants that sounded like stones tumbling in a dry creek bed.

He presses the record button. The light turns solid.

For a long moment, there is only the hum of the refrigerator and the settling of the house’s foundations. Then, he speaks.

"Hello," he says, and the word feels foreign in his own mouth, heavy and strange. "My name is Elias. I am… I am the end."

He pauses, listening to the silence that follows his own voice. It is a terrible silence, not empty, but full of absence. He thinks of his grandmother, who used to sing lullabies in the old tongue, her voice a warm blanket over the cold winters. He thinks of the village square, where the elders would gather to tell stories that had no translation, where the nuances of the language held the entire history of their people in the curve of a vowel. Now, that history is locked in his throat, a key with no lock.

He continues, his voice trembling. "I am recording this because I do not know who will listen. Perhaps no one. Perhaps a researcher, decades from now, will hear this and hear only the sound of a dying man. But I want you to know that it was beautiful. The way the words fit together, like pieces of a mosaic that only made sense when you saw the whole picture. The way we said 'goodbye'—it meant 'until the river changes its course.' We did not say goodbye. We said, 'I will meet you where the water bends.'"

He stops, his breath catching in his chest. He looks around the kitchen, at the familiar clutter of a life lived in the shadow of a language that no longer exists. There is a book on the shelf, a dictionary he has been working on for thirty years, but it is incomplete. It is a monument to a ghost.

"I am afraid," he admits, and the admission costs him more than any other word. "I am afraid that when I die, the sound will be gone forever. That the world will be a little quieter, a little less textured, and no one will know what was lost."

He takes a deep breath, steadying himself. "But I am also grateful. I was the last, but I was not alone. I had the language. I had the memory. And now, I have this. This small, red light. This recording. It is a seed, perhaps. Or a stone. I do not know. But I am leaving it here, in the dark, for whoever comes next."

He releases the button. The light blinks again, waiting. He steps back, his hand hovering over the play button, but he does not press it. He has said what he needed to say. The voicemail is complete. The last speaker of a language has spoken, and the silence that follows is not an ending, but a beginning of a different kind—a quiet, vast, and endless waiting.
