The clock sat on Elias’s workbench, a heavy oak case housing a mechanism that hummed with a quiet, defiant malice. It was a mantel piece, elegant in its brass and glass, but its hands moved with a smooth, unwavering counter-clockwise sweep. The owner, a retired judge named Arthur, had brought it in with a look of profound distress. "It’s broken," Arthur insisted, tapping the glass face. "It’s running the wrong way. It’s a defect."

Elias adjusted his magnifying loupes and lifted the case back. Inside, the gears were pristine. The escapement was balanced. The mainspring was taut. There was no visible damage, no missing tooth, no worn pivot. It was, by every standard of horology, a perfect machine.

"Mr. Arthur," Elias said slowly, wiping a speck of oil from a jewel bearing. "Have you ever considered that it might not be broken? That it might be *correct*?"

Arthur’s brow furrowed. "Correct? Time moves forward. That is the fundamental law of the universe. This thing is a lie."

Elias picked up a small screwdriver and pointed to a tiny, engraved plate near the pendulum. It read: *Memento Mori. The past is the only thing we can truly hold.*

"Who made this?" Arthur asked, his voice losing its judicial edge.

"Your grandfather," Elias replied. "I have records. He was a watchmaker, too. A brilliant one, but... eccentric. He believed that time is not a river, but a loop. He built this clock to remind you that every second you live is a second you can never reclaim. The backward motion is not a malfunction; it is a meditation. It is the sound of memory returning."

Arthur stared at the clock. The hands continued their silent, retrograde journey. For a moment, the room felt heavy with the weight of years. Arthur’s hands, usually steady and precise, trembled slightly as he reached out and touched the cold glass. He remembered his grandfather’s workshop, the smell of sawdust and machine oil, the way the old man would tap the glass and say, "Listen, Arthur. Listen to the past. It is louder than you think."

Elias watched the old man’s face. The anger was fading, replaced by a complex, aching sorrow. The clock was not a defect to be fixed. It was a relic, a testament, a piece of art designed to disrupt the linear narrative of life. To repair it—to make it run forward—would be to erase the grandfather’s final, philosophical gift.

"Leave it," Arthur whispered. "Leave it running."

Elias nodded, closing the case with a gentle click. He did not charge Arthur for the inspection. Some things, he knew, were not meant to be fixed, only understood. The clock ticked on, moving backward into the future, a silent, golden warning that the past was always, always waiting.
