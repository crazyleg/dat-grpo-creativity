The classroom smelled of dry-erase markers and old paper, a scent that had nothing to do with the sterile, recycled air of the International Space Station. I sat at my desk, the wood grain pressing into my palms, and tried to remember how to be a child on Earth.

"Okay, everyone," Mrs. Gable said, tapping her red pen against the whiteboard. "Who wants to tell us what their parents do? Keep it simple. One or two sentences."

I raised my hand. It felt heavy, anchored by gravity in a way it never did up there. When I stood up, my knees wobbled. I looked around the room. Twenty-two faces, all staring. I could see the dust motes dancing in the shafts of sunlight, suspended in a way that reminded me of the zero-g environment, but here, they were just dirt.

"My mom is an astronaut," I said. My voice sounded thin, lacking the resonance of the station’s hum.

A few kids whispered. One boy in the back row snorted. "Does she fly a plane?"

I shook my head. "No. She lives in space."

Mrs. Gable smiled, encouraging. "That’s a start, Leo. Can you tell us more? What does she actually *do*?"

I closed my eyes. I tried to summon the image of my mother. Not the woman who hugged me at the launch site, her face flushed and tight with anxiety, but the woman I saw through the glass of the Cupola. She was floating, her hair a dark halo around her head. She was checking the oxygen scrubbers, her movements precise and slow, like a dancer in a dream. She was talking to mission control, her voice calm, stripped of the fear I knew she felt.

"She fixes things," I said. "The air. The water. The computer systems. She makes sure we don’t die."

The snorting stopped. The room went quiet.

"She also takes pictures," I added, my voice gaining a little strength. "She takes pictures of the Earth. She sends them to me. She says it looks like a blue marble. She says it’s fragile. She says we’re all on the same little rock, spinning in the dark."

I looked at the boy who had snorted. He looked confused, maybe a little afraid. I wanted to tell him that my mother wasn’t a superhero. She was a scientist who was scared of the vacuum, who missed the smell of rain, who cried when she saw the sunrise from orbit. She was a woman who had to learn to pee into a bag and eat food that tasted like cardboard. She was a mother who had to say goodbye to me, and who had to come back.

"She’s coming home next month," I said. "She’s going to be tired. And she’s going to smell like metal and coffee. And she’s going to hug me so hard it hurts."

I sat down. The wood grain pressed into my palms again. I felt the weight of the Earth, solid and undeniable. I didn’t know how to explain the silence of space, the way the stars didn’t twinkle, the way the horizon was a perfect curve. I didn’t know how to explain that my mother was both the most distant person in the world and the closest. I just hoped they understood that she was there, up there, keeping the air breathable, so I could breathe.
